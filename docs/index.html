About the course

The course follows the instructor's textbook, Illustrating Generative AI, with a lab each week and a quarter-long project of your own choosing: a tool for a field you care about, built one milestone at a time. Students from every major are welcome, and no coding is needed: you start from a working tool and make it yours by writing instructions, examples, and tests and by adding your own sources.

Materials

Textbook

Illustrating Generative AI by Chiké Abuah. The course follows its chapters in order. The book is posted on D2L for enrolled students as weekly readings (listed in the schedule): one chapter a week for weeks 1 to 7, two chapters in weeks 8 and 9, and no reading in week 10; each chapter is a runnable notebook, code and real output side by side. The readings are copyrighted and provided for your own use in this course only; please don't share, post, or distribute them.

Slides and labs

One deck and one lab per week, released as the quarter goes. Labs are written to run on your own Ollama; each ends with reflection questions and a submission checklist.

Tools

Ollama for local models (the course uses gemma4, qwen2.5-coder, qwen3, deepseek-r1, qwen3-vl, and nomic-embed-text), uv for Python, and the course's helper module that every lab imports.

The project

Over ten weeks, teams of one to three build a generative AI tool for a field they care about, one milestone a week, with each week's lesson becoming that week's change. No coding is needed. Every team picks one of two paths and starts from a working demo: a Writer, which turns something into a piece of writing (the demo drafts replies to customer reviews for a small bakery), or a Reviewer, which gives feedback on writing (the demo is a writing coach that names each problem with its grammatical term). Teams make it their own by writing instructions, examples, and tests, and by adding their own documents and web pages.

Grading and policies

Labs 30%Project 30%Quizzes 20%Attendance 20%

Labs

One per week, done in class and finished on your own, submitted by pushing to your cs238 GitHub repository and emailing the link. Reflection questions are answered in your own words, without a model.

Quizzes

Nine short quizzes on D2L, Tuesday mornings before class, covering the previous week's lecture and lab. Ten auto-graded questions each, one attempt, fifteen minutes.

Project

Weekly milestones, live demos during the last week of class, and a report. Everyone on a team receives the same milestone marks; the final grade can be adjusted for individual contribution, which the journal records.

Attendance

Class meets twice a week and much of the work happens there. Attendance counts for a fifth of the grade; let me know ahead of time when you have to miss.

Late work

Late work may be accepted, but you must make arrangements with me first. If you need to turn something in late, for any reason, email me before the deadline.

Using AI

This is a course about generative AI, and using it is expected. Two lines don't move: reflection questions and journal entries are yours, written without a model; and every line of code in your repository is yours to explain. Say which assistant you used and what for.

Collaboration and integrity

Collaboration on high-level ideas and approach is encouraged; copying someone else's work is not allowed, and any collaboration must be declared at the top of the submission. Academic misconduct is handled under Walla Walla University's academic integrity policy.

Laptops and phones

Lecture days are device-free: laptops closed, phones away, unless an accommodation says otherwise. Lab days are the exception, since the labs are the point of having a laptop. The reason is other people, not you: in a controlled experiment, students who could see a classmate multitasking on a laptop scored lower on the lecture material even though they weren't using one themselves (Sana, Weston, and Cepeda, 2013), and later work found that a peer's inattention spreads to the students around them, lowering their attention, note-taking, and quiz scores, even when the inattentive student sits behind them (Forrin et al., 2021, 2026; Eder, 1982). If you need a device to learn, talk to me and it's fine.

  • Sana, F., Weston, T., and Cepeda, N. J. (2013). Laptop multitasking hinders classroom learning for both users and nearby peers. Computers & Education, 62, 24–31. doi:10.1016/j.compedu.2012.10.003
  • Forrin, N. D., et al. (2021). Attention spreads between students in a learning environment. Journal of Experimental Psychology: Applied.
  • Forrin, N. D., et al. (2026). Investigating attention contagion between students in a lecture hall.
  • Eder, D. (1982). Peer influence on student attentiveness during classroom lessons.
